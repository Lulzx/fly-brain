# What Emulating an Individual Would Require

The proposal to preserve a mind by computing it is usually stated as one problem. It is at least four, and they fail in different ways. One asks what level of physical description has to be captured. One asks how that description could be measured. One asks how the quantities a measurement cannot see could be inferred. One asks what it would cost to run the result. Only the third and fourth are software problems in the ordinary sense, and the first constrains all the others.

This book is an unusual place to ask the question, because it contains a partial answer. The system described in Chapter 14 is a whole-brain reconstruction of an animal, made executable, given a body, and placed in an environment. It is what a proposal written five years ago would have listed as the first milestone. Having built it, the useful contribution is not to propose it again. It is to report what the attempt revealed about the remaining distance, which is larger in some respects and smaller in others than the milestone framing suggests.

## What has to be captured

The question admits an empirical form. If substituting a model for the tissue leaves behaviour and internal state unchanged, the model captured enough. The candidates form a rough ladder of cost: connectivity alone, then synaptic efficacy, then per-neuron biophysics, then neuromodulation, glia, and slow molecular state. Argument does not settle which rung suffices. Substitution experiments in small nervous systems can, and this project has run some of them.

Their results are consistent and unwelcome. Connectivity alone was not enough. The full-connectome motor mode, in which nerve-cord output drives the leg muscles directly, did not maintain posture. Coordinated stepping had to come from a generator fitted to recorded trajectories. The timing of walk bouts, pauses, and saccades came from a supplied scheduling process whose statistics were taken from the behavioural literature rather than from the graph. Reafferent gain, escape gating, and a steering-adaptation term were all introduced at the interface. A giant-fibre electrical connection had to be added because the packed chemical graph does not contain it.

Several of these additions are informative about the rung rather than merely about the implementation. Per-neuron postsynaptic size scaling and a raised Kenyon-cell threshold were required for calibration, which places some cellular biophysics below the sufficiency line. The octopamine result in Chapter 14 is stronger evidence still: a uniform threshold modulation across 12,851 anatomical targets did not reproduce the starvation phenotype through the network alone, and the observed match depended on a behavioural process that could read the arousal state. Where receptor identity matters and the graph does not carry it, the missing quantity is molecular, not anatomical.

None of this shows that rhythm, bout structure, or state dependence are absent from the animal's wiring. Each negative result is a statement about a tested implementation, and Chapter 15 sets out why a failure can arise from a false interpretation, an unmeasured gain, a missing mechanism, or an unsuitable probe. What the ladder does establish is that the lowest rung has been tried at whole-brain scale and found insufficient, and that the insufficiencies were specific enough to name. That is more progress than a decomposition into questions usually yields.

## What the measurement costs

The only whole-brain structural method available is destructive. Tissue is fixed, sectioned, imaged by electron microscopy, and reconstructed. The imaging is largely a hardware problem; the reconstruction is not. Segmentation, synapse detection, and proofreading at petabyte scale are where the effort has actually been spent. The FlyWire reconstruction of the adult female brain reported 139,255 neurons and 54.5 million synapses after roughly a decade of consortium work, an effort its authors compared to thirty-three scientists working full time for a year. The male central nervous system release used throughout this book contains 165,122 neurons and on the order of 104 million synapses.

Scaling that to a person is arithmetic with a wide error bar. A human brain holds approximately eighty-six billion neurons and something near a hundred and fifty trillion synapses, so the neuron count exceeds the fly’s by a factor of roughly half a million. Imaging one cubic millimetre of cortex at synaptic resolution produced about 1.4 petabytes; extrapolated over a whole brain, the raw imagery is of order a zettabyte. The intermediate target usually named is the mouse, at roughly seventy-one million neurons, where recent work has densely reconstructed a cubic millimetre of visual cortex containing more than 200,000 cells and around half a billion synapses. That is the scale at which human proofreading stops being affordable and the reconstruction pipeline has to be trusted without it.

A point easily lost in these figures is that the pipeline is not a neutral observer. The graph this project executes is thresholded, and the runtime fit in Chapter 14 discards connections below six contacts. Weak connections that might matter collectively are removed before any model is fitted, and a neuron stored as a single node cannot express a computation that occurs in one part of its arbour. The measurement makes choices that no amount of later inference can reverse, which argues for treating uncertainty per synapse as an output of reconstruction rather than as something a modeller estimates afterwards.

## What the scan cannot see

A connectome supplies the graph and not the parameters. Synaptic efficacies, gains, time constants, and modulatory state have to be inferred, which is naturally posed as system identification: fix the structure from the scan, leave the rest free, and fit against recorded function. The method works. The optic-lobe model this project depends on was produced exactly this way, with unknown single-neuron and synapse properties optimised by gradient descent until the network predicted neural responses at single-neuron resolution. A whole-brain integrate-and-fire model built from connectivity and predicted transmitter identity has likewise predicted taste-responsive cells and motor output well enough to be tested optogenetically, and a whole-brain graph has been trained as a controller for a simulated biomechanical fly. The first milestone of any upload roadmap is not merely tractable; it has been passed more than once.

What it has not done is individuate. Every fit of this kind is to species-typical function. flyvis was optimised to detect visual motion, not to reproduce one animal's idiosyncratic tuning. Transmitter assignments are predictions about cell types. The nine global parameters searched in Chapter 14 were scored against a suite of physiological targets drawn from the literature, and the resulting composite near 0.75 measures agreement with what flies in general do. Nothing in that procedure distinguishes this fly from another fly with the same wiring. The models in this book are generic animals, and they were never asked to be anything else.

This is the load-bearing gap, and it is worth stating more sharply than as a guess. Structure plus a finite behavioural record is unlikely to identify a particular individual, because the fitting objective is built from population-level observations and the free parameters are numerous enough to reach them by many routes. The ensemble results in Chapter 6 make the same point in a different setting: baseline-compatible responses can rest on different interactions. If several parameter settings satisfy the observations available, the procedure has not recovered the animal's settings; it has recovered a set consistent with the measurements.

The consequence is an ordering constraint, and it is the substantive claim of this chapter. Dense recording of activity would have to precede the scan, in the same individual, for long enough and across enough states that the functional record carries individual-specific information. A scan taken alone fixes the graph of one animal and leaves its dynamics to be filled in from what animals in general do. Whether any feasible quantity of in vivo recording is sufficient is open, and it is a question that can be attacked at fly scale long before it matters at human scale.

## What it would cost to run

The compute estimate depends entirely on the answer to the first question, which is why it should be quoted last. At the point-neuron level the arithmetic is not discouraging. This project runs 165,122 neurons with a half-millisecond neural step and a trained optic lobe at roughly one fifth of real time per fly on a twelve-core laptop, with several flies at once, in a browser. That figure describes one implementation on one machine, but it establishes an order of magnitude from measurement rather than projection.

Scaling it proportionally by neuron count puts a human-scale point-neuron emulation five to six orders of magnitude above that cost for the same fraction of real time, which is a large cluster or dedicated neuromorphic hardware rather than a workstation, and plausibly not in real time. Sparse event-driven computation helps, since the event-driven wake list in this project's kernel exists for that reason. The estimate is nonetheless the optimistic one, because it presumes the point-neuron abstraction suffices — the assumption the substitution experiments above have already embarrassed. At full biophysical detail the calculation is not worth writing down with current hardware. Every rung added to the ladder in the first section multiplies this figure, so the honest form of the compute question is conditional, and the condition is unresolved.

## A validation that could fail

A roadmap of this kind usually treats validation as the last step. It should be designed first, because it determines what has to be measured, and because it is the step most likely to reveal that the rest was not enough. The test cannot be whether the model behaves plausibly; a generic fly does that. It has to be whether the model is *that* animal.

At fly scale the experiment is specifiable with existing methods. Record activity from two individuals, fit the same connectome separately against each record, and ask whether a held-out behavioural assay can tell the two resulting models apart in the direction that matches the two animals. A positive result would establish that functional recording carries individuating information and would give a first estimate of how much recording is needed. A negative result would be more valuable. It would mean that the parameters recoverable this way are dominated by species-typical structure, and that individuation requires either a different measurement or a level of description the current representation cannot express. Chapter 15's requirement applies in full: the assay and the observable have to be fixed before the comparison, or the result is a consistency check rather than a test.

The same experiment exposes a design choice that the engineering usually leaves implicit. A method that individuates by fitting against a behavioural record can only capture what behaviour reveals, and is therefore a scan-and-copy procedure with a known information bound. A method that replaced tissue incrementally, verifying invariance at each step, would face the sufficiency question in the first section directly and repeatedly. These are different programmes with different failure modes, and the choice between them is not downstream of the software.

## What this book cannot settle

Two questions sit outside the evidence assembled here. Whether a functional copy is a continuation of a person or a new person holding that person's memories is not decided by any measurement described in this chapter, although it does bear on the design: gradual in-place replacement and scan-and-copy answer it differently, and a programme should know which it is pursuing. Whether fidelity at a chosen level of abstraction preserves whatever matters about experience cannot be determined from outside the system, and no result in this book bears on it.

These are noted rather than argued, because the boundary matters. The work reported here is about what a wiring diagram constrains and what experiments must decide. Its contribution to the larger question is narrow and, for that reason, usable: the lowest rung of the substitution ladder has been tested at whole-brain scale and found insufficient in named ways, and the methods that make a connectome predictive have so far produced a generic animal rather than a particular one. Both findings are more useful than a schedule.
