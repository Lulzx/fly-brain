# 52. Slow adaptation in the inhibitory premotor loop

[Doc 51](51-cord-drive.md) found that fatigue in the cord's inhibitory premotor loop makes a
leg's flexor and extensor mutually exclusive. They switch every ~20 ms with exponential-like
dwells, though, so there is no rhythm. The usual fix for a half-centre is a slow process in the
cells that hold the state, one whose time constant sets the period. This screen supplies that
and nothing else new.

## The screen

`cordx7.bend` is `cordx6.bend` with rebound removed. The per-cell variable rebound used now
holds a second, slow adaptation, and only class-1 cells (inhibitory premotor) move it. Each step
it decays by `exp(−dt/τ)`. On each spike it gains the increment, and it adds to the cell's
threshold. Every other cell keeps it at 0. Fast adaptation is the shipped 0.072 mV everywhere.
At increment 0 the variable never moves and adds 0.0, so member 0 is again **byte-identical**
to `cordx4.bend`'s.

| bits | axis | levels |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | slow adaptation time constant, class 1 | 100, 300, 600, 1000 ms |
| 2–3 | slow adaptation per spike, class 1 | 0, 0.5, 1, 2 mV |
| 4–5 | tonic depolarisation, classes 1 and 2 | 0, 2, 4, 6 mV |
| 6 | class 1 → class 1 edge gain | ×1, ×3 |

The inputs are doc 51's. `ext/x7/{real,deg,side}` link to `ext/x6`'s files, and `x7.json` is a
copy of `x6.json`.

The screen repeats doc 51's: strong antagonist switching that only the real wiring makes. Slow
adaptation adds live legs and calls (101 and 24 at increment 0, 180 and 76 at 1 mV). The time
constant hardly matters: across 300, 600 and 1000 ms, the live legs are 163, 169 and 170 and the

0.159 against 0.158 block-shuffled).

Twelve rhythmic leg-windows, against doc 51's two, is the only improvement, and **no member
passes**. The closest is member 120 (τ 100 ms, 1 mV per spike, tonic 6 mV, loop ×3). On the
screen's seed its three right legs share a 330–350 ms period in the first five seconds, and two
of them beat their shuffled nulls. In the second window the periods spread to 340–520 ms and fall
below the nulls, and the fresh seed shows nothing. The median rhythmicity over the real cord's
live windows is 0.105, below the median shuffled maximum of 0.155.

## Why a slow process cannot time this switch

Member 120's legs dwell a median of 20–30 ms per state (10th–90th percentile 10–80 ms), with a
dwell-time coefficient of variation of 0.81–0.98, the same as doc 51. A slow variable with
τ ≥ 300 ms integrates over about fifteen of these flips. Each side spends roughly half of any
window in charge, so both tire at the same rate and the variable carries no information about
which side has been winning. Fatigue can set a period only when a state lasts long enough for
the side holding it to tire *more* than the other side. Here the noise driving the switch, the
Poisson descending and proprioceptive drive across thousands of premotor cells, flips the state
ten times faster than any fatigue builds.

This changes the question. The missing ingredient is not a slower process. It is **hysteresis**:
a state that defends itself against fluctuations long enough for a slow process to act. In the
model class that means one of these:

- **Recurrent excitation inside each half** (the excitatory premotor cells that share a target
  pool), so a winning side holds itself up.
- **Population-level rather than cell-level inhibition between halves.** Raising the class-1 →
  class-1 gain ×3 mostly adds live legs. It does not lengthen dwells, because much of that loop
  is self-inhibition within a hemilineage (doc 51: 19A → 19A is the largest entry).
- **Less private noise per cell.** The Poisson drive here is independent per cell. Correlated or
  tonic drive would not flip the state.

The first two can be measured from the wiring before anything is simulated. Do the excitatory
premotor cells that excite a flexor pool excite one another more than they excite the extensor
pool's? And is the 19A → 13A inhibition stronger, per cell pair, than the 19A → 19A
inhibition? That is the next step.
